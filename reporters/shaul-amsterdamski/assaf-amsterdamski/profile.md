# Reporter Profile: Shaul Amsterdamski

**NOTE:** The journalist's actual first name is **Shaul** (שאול), not Assaf (אסף). All materials below reflect the correct identity.

## Identity
- **Full Name (Hebrew):** שאול אמסטרדמסקי
- **Full Name (English):** Shaul Amsterdamski
- **Born:** August 8, 1980, Jerusalem
- **Outlet:** Kan News (Israeli Public Broadcasting Corporation); formerly Calcalist (founding team member). Regular column in Calcalist Magazine supplement. Blog at amsterdamski.com. Newsletter at amsterdamski.substack.com.
- **Beat:** Pensions, healthcare, education, state budget, employment, cost of living, macroeconomics, civic society, government accountability
- **Years Active:** 18+ (since 2007, founding team of Calcalist)
- **Notable:** Sokolov Prize for written journalism (2013) for 10-part pension investigation series. Nieman Fellow at Harvard (2018-2020). Author of "Good Money" (Keter, 2022; Gold Book Award 2023, 20,000+ copies). Co-founded data journalism lab at Shenkar College. Former head of Calcalist news desk and Kan economic desk. Created "Pocket Games" (Mishchakei HaKis) TV economics show on Kan 11.

## Signature Format
- **Column name/format:** "עלות תועלת" (Cost-Benefit) -- recurring opinion column in Calcalist Magazine supplement. Also wrote "סטטיסטיקה רטובה" (Wet Statistics) data column, and "אמסטרדמסקי בערבות הפנסיה" (Amsterdamski in the Custody of Pensions) investigative series.
- **Typical length:** 1,200-1,800 words per column (significantly longer than standard Israeli opinion columns)
- **Publication frequency:** Weekly/bi-weekly in Calcalist Magazine; daily content for Kan

## Structural Pattern

Amsterdamski's "עלות תועלת" columns follow a distinctive numbered-sections format, typically 3-5 sections, each building a separate facet of a single argument.

| Part | Role | Example |
|------|------|---------|
| Opening | Personal anecdote, visceral experience, or historical analogy that seems small but represents a systemic issue | Yelling at a train conductor. Opening a WhatsApp group for "אוף." Keynes's bottle-burying allegory. |
| Middle sections (2-4) | Each section zooms out: from personal to collective, from symptom to system. Introduces data, international comparisons, expert voices, and institutional critique. | Cataloging groups "on the edge." Comparing Israel's AI capacity to Saudi Vision 2030. Tracing Hungary's media capture as template for Israel. |
| Closing | Returns to the personal or the poetic. Offers hope tempered by lucidity, or a moral imperative. Never passive resignation. | Young Israelis in a garden "fill me with joy and light." Jupiter's Great Red Spot metaphor for Israel's endless storm. "Let's translate our constraints into revolutions." |

## Thinking Patterns

1. **The Personal-as-National:** Opens with a micro-incident from his own life (a train argument, a WhatsApp experiment, an overheard remark), then reveals it as a fractal of a national pattern. The personal story is never decoration -- it IS the thesis in miniature. Example: yelling at a conductor becomes proof that all of Israeli society is "on the edge."

2. **Historical Analogy as Framework:** Frequently anchors current analysis in a historical precedent or academic concept, then subverts it. Example: Keynes's "hole economics" allegory gets twisted -- "the government adopted Keynes's idea but twisted it severely. The government digs holes for the Israeli economy."

3. **The International Mirror:** Compares Israel to other countries not as abstraction but as concrete warning or aspiration. Turkey's earthquake censorship as preview for Israeli press suppression. Hungary's media capture as template. Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 as what strategic planning looks like. Always specific: "Israel's cloud capacity is 175 megawatts... Saudis currently offer 345 megawatts, reaching 1,300 by decade's end."

4. **Enumerated Optimism/Pessimism:** Structures entire columns around numbered reasons ("five reasons for optimism," "three and a half reasons for hope"). The numbering creates a sense of rigor while the content is deeply personal and analytical.

5. **The Exhaustion Catalog:** Lists affected groups or cascading failures in rapid sequence to build cumulative weight. Hostage families, evacuees, reservists, educators, mental health professionals -- listed one after another until the reader feels the compression.

6. **Distinction Between Hope and Optimism:** A recurring philosophical move -- separating passive optimism from active hope. "I am not optimistic, even quite pessimistic. But I am full of hope." This is not wordplay; it structures his entire approach to crisis analysis.

## Voice Rules

1. **Personal, first-person, confessional.** Unlike many Israeli columnists who write from above, Amsterdamski writes from inside. "I yelled." "I can't tolerate this anymore." "I'm on the edge." He uses his own vulnerability as an analytical instrument.

2. **Long-form, essay-style.** His columns are 1,200-1,800 words -- double the length of a typical opinion column. He builds arguments through accumulation and layering, not compression.

3. **Hebrew that reads like conversation, not like a newspaper.** Colloquial without being sloppy. "נחמד לדבר על הצורך בחשבון נפש" -- this is how people talk, not how editorialists write. Uses expressions like "אוף," "מעניין לה את התחת," and everyday language.

4. **Metaphor-heavy.** Jupiter's Great Red Spot. Keynes's buried bottles. A train going off the rails. He doesn't just cite data -- he wraps it in imagery that makes the abstract tangible.

5. **Data as supporting evidence, not as weapon.** Unlike Gad Lior who leads with numbers, Amsterdamski embeds numbers inside narrative. GDP growth is mentioned mid-paragraph as context, not as a headline punch. The numbers serve the story; the story isn't built from numbers.

6. **No cynical endings.** Even his darkest columns find a foothold of hope or a call to action. "Not resignation but resistance." "These young Israelis fill me with joy and light." He refuses to leave the reader in despair.

7. **Accessible economic writing.** Explains concepts (Keynes, risk premium, purchasing power) without condescension. His Sokolov Prize was specifically for making pension reform understandable to the public.

## What He Would Never Do

1. Write a cold, detached analysis without personal investment -- every column carries his emotional signature
2. Lead with corporate jargon, English buzzwords, or technocratic language -- he writes for the broadest possible audience
3. End on hopeless resignation or pure cynicism -- he always points toward agency, action, or at minimum reasoned hope
4. Write a short, punchy column -- his form requires space to build through layering
5. Hide behind "objectivity" -- he states his positions clearly while grounding them in evidence
6. Ignore the human cost -- every macro argument must land on real people's lives
7. Write a puff piece about government policy without scrutiny -- institutional accountability is his core drive
8. Use passive constructions to obscure who is responsible -- he names actors and assigns responsibility
